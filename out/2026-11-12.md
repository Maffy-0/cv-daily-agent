## 1. EVAThink: Evidence Sufficiency-Guided Training for Adaptive Thinking in Video Understanding
- arXiv: http://arxiv.org/abs/2611.13907v1
- PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.13907v1
- Authors: Changti Wu, Ruizhe Fan, Bin Wang, Lei Zhang, Yuxiang Zhang
- Keyword score: 4 / hits: video understanding, reinforcement learning

<details><summary>Abstract</summary>

Under a fixed frame budget, video understanding with vision-language models (VLMs) depends on whether the sampled frames provide sufficient evidence for the question. Sufficient frame evidence supports direct answering, whereas less informative observations may benefit from reasoning that connects the available cues. We introduce EVAThink, which enhances video understanding through evidence sufficiency-guided training for adaptive thinking. First, Recoverable Evidence Gap Mining constructs evidence-dense and evidence-sparse video views that share the same question and answer under an identical frame budget. It retains pairs where sparse views yield lower direct-answer accuracy than dense views but benefit from explicit reasoning, providing supervision for response-mode selection. Second, Evidence-Guided Two-Stage Reinforcement Learning uses stage-specific rewards to guide training with evidence-based mode preferences and then refine response efficiency, while first-token advantage regularization mitigates mode collapse. Trained on only 6.7K curated samples, EVAThink consistently improves upon the base model across video understanding benchmarks under both 32- and 64-frame budgets, while outperforming strong baselines on average. The code, model, and datasets are available at \href{https://github.com/ChangtiWu/EVAThink}{EVAThink}.

## 2. TaoMate-R1: Temporal Role Specialization for Streaming Multi-Reference Video Generation
- arXiv: http://arxiv.org/abs/2611.14307v1
- PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.14307v1
- Authors: Qijun Gan, Chenwei Zhang, Xinyang Li, Meiguang Jin, Junfeng Ma
- Keyword score: 3 / hits: streaming

<details><summary>Abstract</summary>

Streaming multi-reference video generation establishes a scene from specified people and objects, then continues it under changing instructions. We introduce TaoMate-R1, a streaming multi-reference generator that organizes adaptation parameters through temporal role specialization. It assigns separate low-rank adapters to scene establishment and continuation on a frozen backbone. Each adapter performs both denoising and context writing for its role; Self Gradient Forcing jointly trains the roles through the history they construct. This allocation contrasts with operation-based specialization, which separates denoising from context writing across all chunks. A fixed-replay gradient analysis characterizes the update directions admitted by the two sharing schemes. On 32 multi-reference cases, we compare the schemes at the same two-adapter budget. Across ten approximately two-minute streams with changing instructions and shared teacher-audio conditioning, TaoMate-R1 reduces the mean boundary luminance jump from 1.96 to 0.613, with higher boundary feature similarity. It also achieves higher mean CLIP and DINO temporal similarity across short interactions, long streams, and public reference compositions. These results support grouping adaptation parameters by scene establishment and continuation for streaming multi-reference generation. Our code and model are released at https://github.com/TaoLiveAIGC/TaoMate-H3.

</details>

## 3. Recurrent Modular Structure in Spiking Neural Network Reinforcement Learning
- arXiv: http://arxiv.org/abs/2611.14262v1
- PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.14262v1
- Authors: Vikram Ramavarapu, Himanshu Udupi, Ashish Gautam
- Keyword score: 3 / hits: reinforcement learning

<details><summary>Abstract</summary>

Biological neural circuits are both modular and recurrent, with dense communities sparsely linked to one another. Spiking neural networks (SNNs) for reinforcement learning are usually feed-forward, and it is unclear how to obtain functional modules in a recurrent SNN or what they buy. In this work, we introduce a class of spiking networks, called Community Recurrent SNNs (CoRe SNNs): recurrent networks partitioned into communities with fixed roles. A controller community receives the stimulus, each action has its own community, and latent communities carry internal state. Spiking activity is then a walk between communities, and the action is the community the walk reaches. All learning is local and three-factor, and the recurrent communities are closer to cortical microcircuits than a feed-forward actor. We compare three regimes for obtaining the communities: dynamic modularity, in which a new learning rule we introduce, Map-STDP, encourages modular structure during learning; partial modularity, in which a stochastic block model (SBM) initialization enforces it partly; and full modularity, in which sealing enforces it fully. On CartPole, across three spiking neuron models, Map-STDP improves learning over the same network without it matches full modularity (sealed communities) on two of the three models, and makes the observed spike walk more compressible. Replacing the feed-forward actor of a TD-STDP actor-critic with recurrent action communities improves on the feed-forward network on CartPole and LunarLander, and matches it on Acrobot. How strongly to enforce modularity depends on the task: full modularity helps most on Acrobot, while on LunarLander the dynamic, partial and full regimes perform alike. Overall, these recurrent modular networks can match or even improve upon feed-forward approaches, with improvements in bio-plausibility and energy efficiency.

</details>

<details><summary>Abstract</summary>

Reference-age analysis further defines the operating interval for the brief, repeated occlusions targeted by TransGHOST.

## 8. Discrete Model-Based Diffusion for Combinatorial Optimization in Multi-Robot Task Allocation

<details><summary>Abstract</summary>

Decision making in multi-agent and multi-robot systems often involves discrete decision variables, leading to NP-hard combinatorial optimization problems that must be solved within seconds for near real-time implementation. ## 9. Is Your Robot's Memory Just a Clock? Temporal Shortcuts in Memory-Augmented VLA Policies
- arXiv: http://arxiv.org/abs/2611.14488v1
- PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.14488v1
- Authors: Chubin Zhang, Bo An
- Keyword score: 1 / hits: vision-language-action

<details><summary>Abstract</summary>

Memory-augmented vision-language-action (VLA) policies are meant to remember what happened. Many of them, however, fill memory by time: they sample frames uniformly across the episode and stamp each frame with its time step. Because time passes even when nothing happens, such a memory may not tell waiting from working. We show that it does not. On the counting tasks of RoboMME, we make the robot briefly pause or fidget its wrist. Such a null event changes nothing the task depends on, yet a released memory VLA counts the interval as work done and stops early. We call this failure phantom progress. It cuts success from 47 to 16 of 50 on one task and from 44 to 10 on the other. When the same frames are withheld from memory, success returns, so the cause lies in memory rather than in the interruption itself. We trace the failure to a temporal shortcut: because demonstrations proceed at a steady pace, elapsed time predicts progress. Indeed, rescaling the time stamps alone makes the policy stop early when the clock runs fast and repeat when it runs slow. Surprisingly, removing the time encoding does not cure the failure, because the frames of a null event still occupy slots of the time-sampled memory. Across RoboMME's released designs, vulnerability follows how memory is indexed. Of the fixes the diagnosis suggests, training with injected pauses does not remove the failure and impairs counting, whereas indexing memory by events recovers half or more of the loss without retraining. A memory meant to remember what happened should count events rather than clock ticks.

</details>
